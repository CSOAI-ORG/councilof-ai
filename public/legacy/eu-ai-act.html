1. What the EU AI Act actually is (and isn't)

The EU AI Act is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 — a horizontal statute that applies to anyone who places an AI system on the EU market OR whose system's output is used in the EU, regardless of where the provider is located. Think of it as GDPR for AI: extraterritorial, risk-tiered, penalty-heavy.

It is not a certification framework in the ISO-42001 sense. ISO 42001 is about your AI management system; the AI Act is about specific AI systems you place on the market. You can have a perfect AIMS and still breach the AI Act on a single Annex III system. And vice versa.

It is not a ban on AI. Most AI systems are "limited risk" or "minimal risk" — only transparency obligations apply. The hard compliance lift is concentrated in Article 5 prohibitions (8 specific practices) and Annex III high-risk systems (8 categories covering ~15% of enterprise AI deployments).

2. Who it applies to

Roles under the AI Act:

  • Provider — anyone who develops an AI system or has one developed and places it on the EU market under its own name/trademark. Bears the primary obligations.
  • Deployer — anyone who uses an AI system under its own authority (except personal non-professional use). Fewer obligations, but Article 26 still applies.
  • Importer / distributor — parties in the supply chain with specific verification duties.
  • Authorised representative — EU-based party representing non-EU providers.

Extraterritoriality: if you're a US SaaS company whose AI output is used by an EU customer, you are in scope as a provider. There is no "US-only" carve-out.

5. Penalty structure

  • €35M or 7% of global annual turnover — prohibited-practice violations (Art 5).
  • €15M or 3% of turnover — high-risk violations of most operative provisions.
  • €7.5M or 1.5% of turnover — supplying incorrect/incomplete information.
  • Administrative fines scale for SMEs (lower of the two applies).
  • Criminal liability is not introduced by the Act itself, but member states can add it during transposition.
  • Each EU national competent authority enforces nationally — forum shopping is real.

6. The articles that matter most

Article 5 — Prohibited practices

Eight categories. The most commercially relevant are:

  • Art 5(1)(a) subliminal techniques beyond a person's consciousness that materially distort behaviour causing harm — rules out aggressive personalisation patterns in advertising AI.
  • Art 5(1)(b) exploitation of vulnerabilities due to age, disability, or social/economic situation — affects edtech, fintech, gambling-adjacent AI.
  • Art 5(1)(c) social scoring by public authorities with detrimental or unfavourable treatment in unrelated contexts.
  • Art 5(1)(f) emotion inference in workplace and education (except medical/safety reasons).
  • Art 5(1)(g) biometric categorisation inferring race, political opinions, religion, sexual orientation, etc.

Article 9 — Risk management system

You must establish, implement, document, and maintain a continuous risk management system across the AI system's lifecycle. Minimum components: identification, estimation, evaluation, risk-management measures, testing, post-market monitoring feedback.

Article 10 — Data and data governance

Training, validation, and test datasets must be relevant, representative, free of errors, complete. Bias examination for protected characteristics is mandatory. Data governance practices must be documented — where data came from, how it was annotated, how it was cleaned, whether it was augmented, and what biases might remain.

Article 11 — Technical documentation

This is the single most commonly under-delivered requirement. Annex IV specifies 14 fields you must document before CE marking:

  1. General description: intended purpose, developer, version, interaction with hardware/software outside scope, software versions.
  2. Detailed design: methods, training data, relationship between inputs/outputs, pre-determined changes.
  3. Information on monitoring, functioning, control — oversight, accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity.
  4. Description of technical means to comply with Article 13 (transparency to deployers).
  5. Dataset description (Article 10 linkage) — sources, labelling, cleaning, filtering, bias.
  6. Assessment methodology & validation + accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity metrics.
  7. Cybersecurity measures.
  8. Changes during the AI system's lifecycle.
  9. List of harmonised standards + common specifications applied.
  10. EU declaration of conformity.
  11. Post-market monitoring plan (Article 72 linkage).
  12. Risk management plan (Article 9 linkage).
  13. For GPAI components embedded: information required under Article 53.
  14. Any other documentation necessary to demonstrate compliance.

Most technical packs I see cover 3-4 of these fields. Notified Body reviewers reject incomplete packs. The fix: generate all 14 upfront via an AI-BOM + risk-register template.

Article 14 — Human oversight

Effective oversight must be designed into the system. For fully automated decisions, a human must be able to disregard / override / reverse outputs. For assisted decisions, the human must understand the system's capacity and limitations. Requires interface design work, not just policy documents.

Article 43 — Conformity assessment

Two paths:

  • Internal control (Annex VI) — provider self-assesses. Available for MOST Annex III systems under certain conditions.
  • Notified Body assessment (Annex VII) — third-party audit. Required for biometric systems in Annex III point 1 and a few other cases. Currently supply-constrained.

Either path produces a CE mark + EU declaration of conformity + registration in the EU high-risk AI system database.

Article 73 — Serious incident reporting

Providers + deployers must report "serious incidents" to market-surveillance authorities. Without undue delay and within 15 days of awareness (10 days for widespread infringement; 2 days for serious + irreversible). Definitions:

  • Death or serious bodily harm.
  • Serious or irreversible disruption to critical infrastructure management.
  • Fundamental-rights infringement.
  • Serious damage to property or environment.

Runs in parallel to DORA (4h/72h/1mo for financial entities), NIS2 (24h/72h/1mo for essential/important entities), and GDPR (72h). If one incident triggers multiple regimes, plan notification workflows that run in parallel.

7. General-purpose AI (GPAI) models

Articles 51-55 cover foundation models. Two sub-tiers:

  • GPAI models — transparency, copyright protection, training-content summary (Art 53).
  • GPAI models with systemic risk — trigger: 10^25 FLOPs cumulative training compute OR Commission designation. Add: model evaluation, systemic-risk assessment, incident tracking, cybersecurity, Commission cooperation (Art 55).

The Code of Practice for GPAI (published mid-2025) is the practical roadmap for compliance. Signing it is voluntary but reduces burden of proof.

Weeks 1-2 — scoping

  • Identify roles: provider vs deployer vs importer.
  • Designate internal senior responsible person.
  • Map each high-risk system to Article 9 (risk register), Article 10 (data governance), Article 11 (technical documentation), Article 14 (oversight).

Weeks 3-6 — evidence build

  • Generate Annex IV technical documentation pack for each high-risk system (14 fields).

Weeks 7-10 — conformity path

  • Decide Annex VI (internal) vs Annex VII (Notified Body) per system.
  • For NB path: engage Notified Body now — queues are 6-9 months.

  • Register in the EU high-risk AI system database.

  • Post-market monitoring plan + dashboard.
  • Article 73 incident-reporting runbook (incl. DORA + NIS2 + GDPR parallel clocks).
  • Staff AI-literacy training (Article 4).

9. Pre-certification evidence your Notified Body accepts

The Notified Body wants three things per high-risk system:

  1. Clean Annex IV technical documentation pack — all 14 fields populated, cross-linked to test evidence.
  2. Defensible risk management records — Article 9 residual risks, mitigation, sign-offs.
  3. Verifiable continuous monitoring — proof that you didn't just prepare the pack once and walk away.

Cryptographically signed attestations address #3. A HMAC-SHA256 signed cert issued by an independent signing API (like meok-attestation-api.vercel.app) proves:

  • The audit was run on a specific date with a specific score.
  • The payload hasn't been modified since issuance.
  • The cert is verifiable at a public URL without the Notified Body contacting the issuer.

That's the Vanta Trust Center pattern, applied to AI compliance. Pre-certification evidence the NB accepts as preparation, saving weeks of back-and-forth.

10. Common questions

Is voluntary NB engagement faster than waiting for mandatory dates?

Yes. NBs that accept voluntary engagements are running 4-6 month queues vs 6-9 months forecast for the Aug 2026 rush. Pre-certification evidence compresses the NB engagement itself from ~3 months to ~3-4 weeks.

We're a US SaaS — do we really need to do this?

If your AI output is used by EU customers: yes. Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Mistral, Cohere all have EU AI Act compliance programmes. Providers cannot delegate the obligation to deployers.

What's the minimum viable compliance posture for a Series A?

Risk classification completed (Art 6 + Annex III mapping). Annex IV pack at ~60% complete. Signed quarterly self-attestation posture. Named senior accountable person. Documented Article 14 human oversight. Incident-reporting runbook. Article 4 literacy training log.
