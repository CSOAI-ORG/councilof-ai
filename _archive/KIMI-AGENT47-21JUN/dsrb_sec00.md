# Executive Summary

## The Opportunity: AI Governance Meets Digital Operational Resilience

The Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) is now in force. Since 17 January 2025, over 22,000 financial entities across the EU — from global systemically important banks to building societies, payment institutions, and crypto-asset service providers — must maintain ICT risk management frameworks, incident reporting, third-party risk registers, and resilience testing [^1^]. The European Supervisory Authorities have designated 19 Critical ICT Third-Party Providers (CTPPs) including AWS, Microsoft, Google Cloud, IBM, Oracle, SAP, Swift, and Bloomberg, placing them under direct oversight [^2^].

The market is vast: DORA compliance TAM sits between EUR 110 billion and EUR 330 billion, at EUR 5–15 million per entity across 22,000 organisations [^3^]. Beneath these figures lies a structural market failure — and the most significant strategic opening for an AI-native governance platform in the current regulatory cycle.

## Zero Competition at the AI Governance × DORA Intersection

Every established DORA vendor — OneTrust (14,000+ customers), SAI360, MetricStream, Regnology — operates without integrated AI governance. Every AI governance platform — Holistic AI and emerging entrants — has zero DORA functionality [^4^]. This structural segmentation has persisted despite accelerating regulatory convergence.

That convergence is now law. BaFin's January 2026 guidance requires AI systems within financial institutions **must** be embedded within DORA ICT risk management frameworks [^5^]. AI models and automated decision systems must be covered by the same incident reporting, testing, and third-party risk protocols as core banking infrastructure. CSOAI is the only platform architecturally capable of satisfying both simultaneously.

## Five Universal Requirements, Ten Global Frameworks

Analysis of ten major frameworks — DORA, CBEST, TIBER-EU, NIST CSF 2.0, FFIEC, APRA CPS 230, MAS TRM, and BAIT — reveals convergence on five universal requirements: board accountability for ICT risk, threat intelligence, third-party risk management, incident classification and reporting, and regular resilience testing [^6^]. These pillars are non-negotiable across every framework.

CSOAI's architecture maps to all five. The BFT Council delivers cryptographically verifiable board accountability. The Worm Hive mesh generates continuous threat monitoring. The Pheromone Matrix classifies incidents across DORA's seven criteria — client impact, duration, data loss, criticality, geographic spread, economic impact, reputational impact — using biological signalling heuristics that correlate directly with regulatory schemas [^7^]. MCP server catalogues with cryptographic attestation automate third-party risk documentation. This is architectural alignment, not retrofit.

## The Entry Point: Register of Information

The most immediate opportunity is DORA Article 28's Register of Information (RoI). EBA dry-run data shows 46% of entities identified the RoI as their greatest compliance challenge — only 6.5% passed [^8^]. The RoI demands structured documentation of thousands of ICT third-party relationships, contract terms, risk assessments, and dependencies. Legacy GRC tools fail here; CSOAI's MCP server catalogue automates it. Six quick wins are achievable within 0–30 days, leveraging capabilities with 65% native DORA alignment [^9^].
