## 3. The DSRB Ecosystem — Partners, Providers & Gaps

### 3.1 What "DSRB" Actually Means

#### 3.1.1 DSRB as a Commercial Category Does Not Exist Formally — It Refers to the Functional Ecosystem of Digital Resilience Services

Search for the acronym "DSRB" in regulatory databases, market research reports, or trademark registries, and you will arrive at a singular discovery: **the term does not denote a commercial category**. The acronym refers to the *Defence, Security and Resilience Bank* Development Group — a proposed multilateral institution for sovereign defense financing across allied nations [^852^]. It is a policy construct, not a market segment.

The functional equivalent — the ecosystem of services banks use to build digital operational resilience — has no unified label. Industry analysts, regulatory bodies, and vendors refer to it through a fragmented vocabulary: operational resilience services, RegTech, GRC platforms, or simply "DORA compliance." This fragmentation reveals a market still organizing itself, where category boundaries are porous and vendor positioning remains fluid. For a firm like CSOAI, this ambiguity is strategic terrain. The first mover that names the category, defines its taxonomy, and architects its value proposition will shape purchasing decisions across **22,000 regulated entities** for the decade ahead [^917^].

The structural complexity of this market is best understood through its layered architecture. No single provider spans all layers. Banks do not buy resilience from one vendor — they stitch it together from multiple provider classes, each with its own procurement cycle, decision-maker, and technical standards. This fragmentation explains why DORA compliance is so operationally burdensome: spending is split across risk advisory (CRO budget), technology platforms (CIO/CTO budget), cloud infrastructure (infrastructure budget), and consulting (transformation budget). A solution that cuts across these silos has no natural budget line. It must create one.

#### 3.1.2 The Five-Layer Ecosystem

The digital resilience services ecosystem flows from regulatory authority to financial entity across five interconnected layers. Understanding this stack is essential for identifying where CSOAI's AI governance layer fits — and which players at each level represent the highest-value partnership targets.

| Layer | Role | Representative Providers | What Banks Procure |
|-------|------|-------------------------|--------------------|
| **L1: Regulatory Authorities** | Rule-setting, supervision, penalties | ECB, EBA, ESMA, EIOPA, BaFin, ACPR, DNB | Not procured — banks are *regulated by* this layer |
| **L2: Big 4 Consultancies** | Framework design, gap assessment, roadmaps | Deloitte, KPMG, EY, PwC, Accenture | DORA readiness assessments; ICT risk redesign; managed compliance |
| **L3: Technology Providers** | GRC, workflow, and security platforms | OneTrust, ServiceNow, IBM OpenPages, SAI360, MetricStream | Register of Information automation; incident workflows; TPRM platforms |
| **L4: Critical Third Parties** | Cloud, core banking, infrastructure under direct oversight | AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, IBM, Oracle, SAP, DT [^882^] | Hyperscaler cloud; core banking; payment rails — now ESMA-supervised |
| **L5: Financial Entities** | Regulated institutions that must comply | 22,000+ EU banks, insurers, investment firms, CASPs [^917^] | Internal capability build; Layers 2-4 services; staff augmentation |

This model explains the friction. A Tier 2 bank must simultaneously interpret regulatory guidance from Layer 1, engage Deloitte or KPMG from Layer 2, deploy OneTrust or ServiceNow from Layer 3, negotiate CTPP oversight with AWS or IBM from Layer 4, and orchestrate internal teams across risk, IT, legal, and procurement in Layer 5. Each interface introduces compliance risk. **No single vendor provides a unified control plane across all five layers**. This is the gap CSOAI is designed to fill.

### 3.4 The Critical Market Gap

#### 3.4.1 46% of Banks Struggle with the Register of Information; Only 6.5% Passed the ESA Dry-Run

The most immediate evidence of market failure lies in the Register of Information. **Deloitte found that 46% of financial entities name the ROI as their most challenging DORA requirement** [^855^]. The reasons are structural: 92% of institutions outsource IT operations, and 77% rely on cloud infrastructure [^851^]. Contracts are scattered across procurement teams and subsidiaries. Subcontractor visibility — the fourth and fifth parties beneath the primary vendor — is effectively nonexistent [^850^].

The ESAs' 2024 dry-run testing exposed catastrophic readiness. **Only 6.5% of firms passed all 116 data quality checks**, and Excel-based submissions had a **94% failure rate** [^854^]. These are not marginal shortfalls. They represent market-wide incapacity to execute DORA's most basic data requirement. The supervisory pattern is now established: regulators check ROI completeness first, then CIF classification consistency, then contract provisions against the Article 30 mandatory list [^852^]. Banks that fail at step one face immediate escalation — and fines of up to 2% of global turnover or EUR 10 million [^852^].

#### 3.4.2 EUR 5–15 Million DORA Spend per Institution × 22,000 Entities = EUR 110–330 Billion TAM

McKinsey research found that institutions plan to spend between **EUR 5 million and EUR 15 million on DORA compliance** [^853^]. With **22,000 entities in scope** [^917^], the total addressable market ranges from **EUR 110 billion to EUR 330 billion** — a figure that places digital resilience among the largest compliance-driven technology markets in the history of European financial regulation. This is not one-time spending: DORA requires annual ROI submissions, recurring resilience testing, ongoing incident reporting, and perpetual third-party monitoring.

The spending is already flowing. Almost **40% of entities dedicate more than 7 FTEs** to DORA programs [^851^]. **90% of companies are increasing third-party risk management investment** [^853^]. The November 2025 CTPP designations — placing 19 hyperscalers under direct ESMA/EBA supervision [^882^] — added a new compliance layer for every bank dependent on these providers. The first TLPT deadline of January 2028 is approaching, driving further investment in testing and red team capabilities. Every budget line is expanding simultaneously.

#### 3.4.3 AI-Enabled Attacks Grew 72% in 2025; BaFin Mandates AI in DORA Frameworks

The threat environment compounds the compliance burden. **AI-related cybersecurity attack activity increased 72% in 2025**, with global cybersecurity spending reaching **$240 billion** — DORA explicitly cited as the driver [^856^]. Banks must defend against AI-augmented attacks while governing their own AI systems, a dual challenge that no existing vendor portfolio addresses.

BaFin's January 2026 guidance clarified the architecture: **AI-based systems must be fully embedded into DORA-compliant ICT risk management frameworks** — not treated as a separate regime [^850^]. Model monitoring, access controls, and resilience testing for AI applications are now mandatory components of DORA compliance. Nordic NCAs have similarly prioritized digital resilience as a top supervisory focus for 2026 [^850^].

This convergence creates a timing advantage that will not last. The market divides into DORA compliance platforms (zero AI governance), AI governance platforms (zero DORA integration), cybersecurity vendors (zero governance), and consultancies (no proprietary technology). The Deloitte/BigID December 2025 webinar poll confirmed the void: **0% of attendees reported full compliance across DORA, NIS2, and the AI Act**, and 28% had not started [^848^]. ACE + Company's October 2025 analysis validated the structural overlap: the same logging, monitoring, incident reporting, and third-party oversight requirements appear across all three regimes, yet banks maintain separate teams, systems, and audit trails for each [^846^].
