📖 Preamble — The Meadow, the Barons, and the Charter

On 15 June 1215, at Runnymede, a meadow beside the River Thames in Surrey, England, King John of England affixed his great seal to a charter of liberties that would become the foundational document of constitutional sovereignty in the English-speaking world. The Magna Carta — Latin for "Great Charter" — was the result of a brutal political crisis in which a coalition of rebellious English barons, supported by Stephen Langton the Archbishop of Canterbury, forced the King to acknowledge that royal authority was bounded by law.

King John (reigned 1199–1216) had been a disastrous monarch. He lost virtually all the Plantagenet possessions in France, including Normandy and Anjou, in the disastrous wars against Philip II of France. To pay for these wars and for failed attempts to recover his losses, he levied extortionate taxes on his barons, abused the forest laws, confiscated estates, and conducted arbitrary justice. In 1213 he had even accepted the English crown as a papal fief from Pope Innocent III, deeply alienating the barons who saw him as a vassal of Rome.

In May 1215, the barons renounced their feudal allegiance and marched on London. They occupied the city. They drafted a document called the "Articles of the Barons" — which became the substantive core of the eventual Charter — and presented it to the King. Archbishop Langton mediated. On 15 June 1215, at the meadow called Runnymede ("the meadow of the council"), King John set his seal to the Charter.

📜 The 63 Clauses of Magna Carta (1215)

The original 1215 Charter contained 63 clauses. Many dealt with feudal disputes of little modern relevance — the weights of wool, the rights of wardship over heirs, the size of fish weirs on the Thames. But a small number of clauses became foundational for constitutional government, and three clauses remain on the statute book in England to this day (Clauses 1, 9, and 29).

Clause 1 — The Church

"FIRST, THAT WE HAVE GRANTED TO GOD, and by this our present Charter have confirmed, for us and our heirs for ever, that the Church of England shall be free, and shall have all her whole Rights and Liberties inviolable for ever."

This opening clause — placing the Church above royal interference — was a precondition for the barons' support of the Charter. It was the first of the freedoms to be enumerated.

Clause 9 — The City of London

"THE CITY OF LONDON shall have all the old Liberties and free Customs, as well by Land as by Water. Moreover, we will and grant, that all other Cities, Boroughs, Towns, and the Barons of the Cinque Ports, and all other Ports, shall have all their Liberties and free Customs."

The City of London — which had been the barons' chief urban ally — secured its ancient privileges.

Clause 12 — No Tax Without Consent

"No Scutage or Aid shall be imposed on our Kingdom, unless by common Counsel of our Kingdom, except for ransoming our Person, making our eldest Son a Knight, and once for marrying our eldest Daughter."

This is the foundational clause of parliamentary taxation. "No taxation without representation" descends directly from this clause — the barons demanded that the King could not levy taxes (called "scutage" or "aid") without the common counsel of the realm. The 13th clause similarly constrained the King's power to demand feudal obligations.

Clause 20 — Fines Proportional to Offence

"A free Man shall not be amerced [fined] for a small Fault, but according to the Degree of the Fault; and for a great Fault after the same manner, according to the Magnitude of it, saving his Contenement [livelihood]."

Proportional punishment — a foundational principle of common law.

Clause 28 — Royal Officers Punishable

"No Constable or other Bailiff of ours shall take Corn or other Chattels of any Man without instantly tendering him Money therefore, unless he can presently shew the King's Writ or Warrant for so doing."

Even the King's officials must pay for what they take.

Clause 30 — No Horses, Wood, Cart Taken Without Consent

"No Sheriff nor Bailiff of ours, nor any other Person, shall take the Horses or Carts of any free Man for Carriage, without the Consent of the said free Man."

Clause 39 — The Soul of the Charter

"NO FREE MAN shall be seized, imprisoned, dispossessed, outlawed, exiled or ruined in any way, except by the lawful judgement of his peers or by the law of the land."
"Nullus liber homo capiatur, imprisonetur, aut disseisiatur, aut utlagetur, aut exuletur, aut aliquo modo destruatur, nisi per legale judicium parium suorum vel per legem terrae."

This is the most famous clause — and the foundational principle of due process. The clause remained on the English statute book until 2015 (when it was moved to a schedule of the new Bills of Rights Act 1689 reaffirmation). It is the foundation of the Anglo-American conception of judicial independence and the rule of law.

Clause 40 — "To no one will we sell, deny, or delay right or justice"

"To no one will we sell, deny, or delay right or justice."

The second of the two clauses still on the statute book. The Crown will not sell or deny justice. This is the foundational anti-corruption clause.

Clause 61 — The Council of Twenty-Five

"The Barons shall choose five and twenty Barons of the Kingdom, whom they please, who shall be bound with all their might, by observing and causing to be observed the peace and liberties we have granted and confirmed to them by this our present Charter; so that if we, our Justiciary, our Bailiffs, or any of our officers shall have transgressed against anyone in any respect, or shall have broken any of the Articles of this Peace or of this Charter, and the offence shall have been shewn to four Barons of the said five and twenty, those four Barons shall come before us, or our Justiciary if we be out of the Kingdom, and laying open the grievance, shall petition that we cause the transgression to be redressed without delay."

The clause established a committee of 25 barons who could collectively override the King by force if he violated the Charter. Pope Innocent III annulled this clause within months. But the principle — that sovereign authority is subject to lawful restraint — survived.

🔁 The Reissues of Magna Carta (1216, 1217, 1225, 1297)

The original 1215 Charter was never observed — civil war resumed within months, and King John died in October 1216. But the principle was reaffirmed:

The 1225 reissue — by Henry III upon reaching the age of majority — is the legally operative version. It was entered into the English statute roll and confirmed by Edward I in 1297.

🇺🇸 Magna Carta's American Influence

The American colonial charters explicitly referenced Magna Carta. The Fifth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution (1791) — "nor shall any person be... deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law" — is a direct descendant of Clause 39. The Sixth Amendment's right to a speedy trial by peers is a descendant of Clause 39's "lawful judgement of his peers."

The framers of the U.S. Constitution, in the Federalist Papers, cited Magna Carta as the foundational document of English constitutional liberty. The Mayflower Compact (1620), the Declaration of Rights of the Stamp Act Congress (1765), the Declaration of Independence (1776), and the U.S. Bill of Rights (1791) all descend from the principle of Magna Carta.
