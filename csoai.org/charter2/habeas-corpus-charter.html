📖 Preamble — The Stuart Crisis of Detention

By the late 1670s, Charles II of England (reigned 1660–1685) had grown increasingly hostile to the Whig opposition in Parliament. The King's ministers — particularly the Earl of Danby and later Lord Chancellor Finch — used the Star Chamber (until 1641 abolished) and the revived Court of King's Bench to detain political opponents indefinitely without trial. Suspected dissidents, nonconformists, and Catholic recusants were imprisoned in the Tower of London, in gaols across the realm, and aboard prison ships in the Thames on the strength of warrants signed by the King or his ministers alone. No trial. No jury. No release.

The crisis came to a head in 1677 when the radical Whig leader Anthony Ashley Cooper, 1st Earl of Shaftesbury (1621–1683), then serving as Lord Chancellor, allied with Sir William Jones and the Member for Bramber, Sir Francis Winnington, to draft a bill that would force the Crown to either charge detainees or release them. Shaftesbury had himself spent years evading royal arrest during the Popish Plot furore (1678), and the bill was a direct response to the perceived arbitrariness of the Stuart executive.

The Bill passed through Parliament in three readings between February and May 1679. On 27 May 1679, Charles II gave the royal assent despite his misgivings — the statute is officially "An Act for the better securing the Liberty of the Subject, and for Prevention of Imprisonments beyond the Seas," known to history as the Habeas Corpus Act 1679 (31 Cha. II c. 2). It remains the foundational statute of English liberty against arbitrary state power.

📜 The Five Sections of the Habeas Corpus Act 1679

The Act is short, blunt, and procedural. It contains five substantive sections, plus a preamble and enforcement clause. Reproduced here from the statute roll:

Section I — The Writ Itself

"Whereas great delays have been used by sheriffs, gaolers, and other officers to whose custody any of the King's subjects have been committed for criminal or supposed criminal matters, in making returns of writs of habeas corpus to them directed, by holding persons in custody not charged with any crime, the Lord Chancellor and the Lord Keeper of the Great Seal of England for the time being, and the Chancellor and Keeper of the Great Seal of Ireland, and every judge of the King's Bench and Common Pleas for the time being, and the Barons of the Exchequer of the degree of the coif for the time being, and every person being the King's Serjeant-at-Law attending the said Court of King's Bench for the time being, in the respective vacations of the said courts, upon sight of the said writ or duplicate, signed by the person that awarded the same, shall, within three days after receipt thereof (Sunday excepted), award and sign one or more writ or writs of habeas corpus, under the seal of the court whereof he or they shall then be one of the judges, unto the person or persons in whose custody the party so committed or detained shall be, returnable immediately before the said Lord Chancellor, etc."

The first section compels any of the senior judges to issue the writ on demand, even during the legal vacation (summer recess). Three days, Sundays excluded. No discretion.

Section II — Six-Day Delivery for Felony, Twenty-Day for Misdemeanour

"And to the intent that no sheriff, gaoler, or other officer may pretend ignorance of the import of any such writ of habeas corpus, every such officer shall, within three days after the receipt of such writ (Sundays excepted), pay obedience thereunto, and shall make return of the same to the Lord Chancellor, etc., and shall bring or cause to be brought the body of the party so committed or detained, before the said Lord Chancellor, etc., and shall likewise certify the true cause of his detention, unless the commitment of the said party be for treason or felony plainly and specially expressed in the warrant of commitment: in which case, the said party shall be brought before the said Lord Chancellor, etc., within twenty days after the delivery of the said writ, to be dealt with according to law."

The crucial numbers: six days for misdemeanour, twenty days for treason or felony. If the gaoler cannot produce the detainee and a certified cause, the prisoner must be bailed or discharged.

Section III — No Removal Beyond the Seas

"And if any person or persons shall be or stand committed or detained as aforesaid, for any crime unless for felony or treason plainly expressed in the warrant of commitment, in the vacation time, and out of the term, it shall and may be lawful to and for the person or persons so committed or detained (other than persons convict or in execution by legal process) to appeal or complain to the Lord Chancellor, etc., who, upon sight of the said writ or duplicate, signed by the person that awarded the same, shall, within three days (Sundays excepted), award and sign one or more writ or writs of habeas corpus, under the seal of the court whereof he or they shall then be one of the judges, returnable immediately before the person or persons so authorized as aforesaid; and being thereupon returned, shall, within two days after, examine and determine the legality of such imprisonment, and discharge the party if it shall appear to be illegal."

This is the section that made the Act infamous — it forbade the Crown from shipping prisoners overseas (e.g., to the American colonies, to Tangier, or to the plantations). The detention must be in a known English or Irish gaol.

Section IV — Penalty for Non-Compliance

"Every person and persons upon whom any such writ of habeas corpus shall be served, and who shall neglect or refuse to make returns as aforesaid, or to bring the body or bodies of the prisoner or prisoners according to the command of the said writ, shall, for the first offence, be punished by fine and imprisonment at the discretion of the court; and for the second offence, by fine and imprisonment as aforesaid, and shall moreover be incapable to hold or enjoy any office or place of trust or profit under the King."

The penalty for refusing the writ: fine, imprisonment, and permanent disqualification from Crown office on second offence.

Section V — No Re-Imprisonment for the Same Cause

"No subject of the King shall be sent prisoner into Scotland, Ireland, Jersey, Guernsey, Tangier, or into any parts, garrisons, islands, or places beyond the seas, which are or hereafter shall be within or without the dominions of the King, and every such imprisonment is hereby declared to be illegal; and if any of the King's subjects shall hereafter be so imprisoned, every such person may for every such offence maintain, by his Majesty's Writ of Habeas Corpus, an action against the person or persons by whom he or she shall be so committed, detained, imprisoned, sent, or transported, contrary to the true meaning of this Act, in which action the plaintiff shall recover treble costs beside his damages."

Final teeth: treble damages against any officer who violates the Act. The Habeas Corpus Act 1679 is one of the few English statutes to provide for exemplary damages.

⚖️ Famous Cases — How the Writ Was Used

The Habeas Corpus Act 1679 was immediately tested:

William Prynne (1637 / revived use 1679)

The Puritan pamphleteer had been imprisoned by Star Chamber in 1637 for his tract Histriomastix; the writ was the mechanism by which his 1679 release (and that of many nonconformists held without trial) was forced. Prynne lived until 1669, but the principle he had championed became operative in the 1680s.

John Lilburne (Lilburn, 1649, then 1679 spirit)

Lilburne had been the first famous English habeas corpus litigant in the 1640s — the Leveller whose repeated writs of habeas corpus against Cromwell's Council of State established the right in the 1649 Act. The 1679 statute is in many ways the formalisation of the Lilburne precedent, which had been in constitutional limbo since the Restoration.

The Seven Bishops (1688 — the application that mattered)

In 1688, Archbishop Sancroft and six other bishops petitioned James II against the Declaration of Indulgence. They were committed to the Tower. Their counsel — including the future Lord Somers — applied for habeas corpus. The Court of King's Bench, sitting in Westminster Hall, granted the writ. On 29 June 1688, the bishops were acquitted by the jury in a verdict that is widely held to have made the Glorious Revolution inevitable.

Sir John Fenwick (1696)

The Jacobite conspirator was attainted by Act of Parliament (the only way around habeas corpus in cases of state emergency). The Crown's inability to detain him without trial under the 1679 Act is the reason the attainder process was revived.

🏛️ Suspension and Re-Enactment

The Act has been suspended by Parliament seven times in British history — most notoriously in 1794 (against radicals during the French Revolutionary wars) and most recently in Northern Ireland 1971–1975. Each suspension is a constitutional moment: the citizen's protection against arbitrary state power is removed only by the deliberative act of Parliament.

The Habeas Corpus Act 1816 (56 Geo. III c. 100) extended the 1679 Act to all cases of detention, not just criminal matters, and made the writ returnable in court rather than at chambers. The Habeas Corpus Act 1862 removed all doubts about the Crown's power to detain British subjects in foreign territories.

🇺🇸 The American Adoption

The U.S. Constitution, Article One, Section Nine, Clause Two, provides: "The Privilege of the Writ of Habeas Corpus shall not be suspended, unless when in Cases of Rebellion or Invasion the public Safety may require it." The Suspension Debates of 1861 (Lincoln's suspension during the Civil War), Ex parte Merryman (1861), and Ex parte Milligan (1866) are all direct descendants of the 1679 statute.
