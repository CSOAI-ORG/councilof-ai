📜 Preamble — The Most Important Privacy Law Ever Drafted

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 27 April 2016 on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data. It entered into force on 24 May 2016 and applied from 25 May 2018. The GDPR replaced the 1995 Data Protection Directive (Directive 95/46/EC) and is the most consequential data protection law in the world.

The GDPR was developed in response to the rapid growth of the internet, social media, mobile computing, and cloud computing. The 1995 Directive had become outdated — it was enacted before the existence of Facebook, Twitter, smartphones, cloud storage, or AI. The GDPR updates the law for the 21st century digital age.

The GDPR was drafted over four years (2012–2016). The European Commission published a draft in January 2012. The European Parliament adopted its position in March 2014. The Council of the European Union adopted its position in June 2015. After trilogue negotiations, the final text was agreed in December 2015. The European Parliament adopted the final text on 14 April 2016, the Council adopted it on 8 April 2016, and it was signed by the Presidents of the Parliament and the Council on 14 April 2016.

The GDPR has 11 chapters and 99 articles. It is the world's most-cited privacy regulation. The United Kingdom adopted the UK GDPR as a retained EU law on 31 December 2020, with technical changes.

📖 Chapter 1 — General Provisions (Articles 1-4)

Article 1 — Subject Matter and Objectives

"(1) This Regulation lays down rules relating to the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and rules relating to the free movement of personal data. (2) This Regulation protects fundamental rights and freedoms of natural persons and in particular their right to the protection of personal data. (3) The free movement of personal data within the Union shall be neither restricted nor prohibited for reasons connected with the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data."

Article 2 — Territorial Scope

"This Regulation applies to the processing of personal data wholly or partly by automated means, and to the processing other than by automated means of personal data which form part of a filing system..."

Article 3 — Definitions

Defines "personal data", "processing", "controller", "processor", "profiling", "consent", "personal data breach", "main establishment", and 11 other terms.

📖 Chapter 2 — Principles (Articles 5-11)

Article 5 — Principles Relating to Processing of Personal Data

"1. Personal data shall be: (a) processed lawfully, fairly and in a transparent manner in relation to the data subject ('lawfulness, fairness and transparency'); (b) collected for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes and not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes ('purpose limitation'); (c) adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed ('data minimisation'); (d) accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date ('accuracy'); (e) kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data are processed ('storage limitation'); (f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorised or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction or damage, using appropriate technical or organisational measures ('integrity and confidentiality')." "2. The controller shall be responsible for, and be able to demonstrate compliance with, paragraph 1 ('accountability')."

Article 6 — Lawfulness of Processing (The 6 Lawful Bases — VERBATIM)

"1. Processing shall be lawful only if and to the extent that at least one of the following applies: (a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of his or her personal data for one or more specific purposes; (b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the data subject's request prior to entering into a contract; (c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject; (d) processing is necessary in order to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person; (e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller; (f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular where the data subject is a child." "2. Member State law may provide for more specific provisions to adapt the application of the rules of this Regulation for compliance with legal obligations or for the performance of tasks carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller..."

These 6 lawful bases are the foundation of every personal data processing in the EU. Every controller must identify at least one lawful basis before processing any personal data.

Article 7 — Conditions for Consent

"1. Where processing is based on consent, the controller shall be able to demonstrate that the data subject has consented to processing of his or her personal data. 2. If the data subject's consent is given in the context of a written declaration which also concerns other matters, the request for consent shall be presented in a manner which is clearly distinguishable from the other matters..."

Article 8 — Child's Consent in Information Society Services

"1. Where point (a) of Article 6(1) applies, in relation to the offer of information society services directly to a child, the processing of the personal data of a child shall be lawful where the child is at least 16 years old..."

Article 9 — Processing of Special Categories of Personal Data

Generally prohibited except where explicit consent, employment law, vital interests, public interest, medical purposes, etc. Includes racial origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data, health data, sex life, sexual orientation.

Article 10 — Processing of Data on Criminal Convictions and Offences

Allowed only under the control of official authority or when authorised by Union or Member State law.

Article 11 — Processing Not Requiring Identification

"1. The controller shall not be obliged to maintain, acquire or process additional information to identify the data subject for the sole purpose of complying with this Regulation..."

📖 Chapter 3 — Rights of the Data Subject (Articles 12-23)

These are the most-cited articles in practice. Every EU citizen has these 8 rights:

Article 13 — Information to Be Provided Where Personal Data Are Collected from the Data Subject

The controller must provide: identity and contact details, purposes, legal basis, recipients, retention period, rights, complaints.

Article 15 — Right of Access by the Data Subject

"1. The data subject shall have the right to obtain from the controller confirmation as to whether personal data concerning him or her are being processed, and, where that is the case, access to the personal data and the following information: (a) the purposes of the processing; (b) the categories of personal data concerned; (c) the recipients or categories of recipient to whom the personal data have been or will be disclosed... (d) the envisaged retention period... (e) the existence of the right to request from the controller rectification or erasure of personal data or restriction of processing... (f) the right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority..."

Article 16 — Right to Rectification

Data subject has the right to obtain rectification of inaccurate personal data without undue delay.

Article 17 — Right to Erasure ('Right to Be Forgotten')

"1. The data subject shall have the right to obtain from the controller the erasure of personal data concerning him or her without undue delay and the controller shall have the obligation to erase personal data without undue delay where one of the following grounds applies: (a) the personal data are no longer necessary in relation to the purposes for which they were collected or otherwise processed; (b) the data subject withdraws consent on which the processing is based... (c) the data subject objects to the processing... (d) the personal data have been unlawfully processed; (e) the personal data have to be erased for compliance with a legal obligation in Union or Member State law..."

Article 18 — Right to Restriction of Processing

In certain circumstances, the data subject can require the controller to restrict processing (i.e., store but not process).

Article 20 — Right to Data Portability

"1. The data subject shall have the right to receive the personal data concerning him or her, which he or she has provided to a controller, in a structured, commonly used and machine-readable format and have the right to transmit those data to another controller without hindrance from the controller to which the personal data have been provided..."

This is the foundational article for sovereign data portability. SOV3 implements Article 20 by allowing every sovereign citizen to export their i-character in a structured, machine-readable format.

Article 21 — Right to Object

The data subject has the right to object to processing based on legitimate interests or public interest, including profiling.

Article 22 — Automated Individual Decision-Making, Including Profiling

"1. The data subject shall have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her, except as provided in paragraphs 2 and 3."

Article 22 is the foundation of the SOV3 12-around-1 BFT Council — every sovereign action is judged by the council, not by an algorithm alone. Automated decisions that "significantly affect" a citizen are subject to human review by the BFT Council.

Article 23 — Restrictions

Member State law may restrict scope of the obligations and rights to safeguard national security, defence, public security, etc.

📖 Chapter 4 — Controller and Processor (Articles 24-43)

Defines the obligations of data controllers and data processors. Key provisions: data protection by design and by default (Art 25), security of processing (Art 32), breach notification (Art 33), Data Protection Impact Assessments (Art 35), Data Protection Officer (Art 37).

📖 Chapter 5 — Transfers to Third Countries (Articles 44-50)

Article 46 — Transfers Subject to Appropriate Safeguards

Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) and Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs) may be used for transfers.

Article 49 — Derogations for Specific Situations

Explicit consent, contract performance, public interest, legal claims, vital interests, public register.

📖 Article 50 — The Sovereignty Clause (THE FOUNDATIONAL ARTICLE 50)

"The Commission may decide, in accordance with the procedure referred to in Article 93(2), that a third country, a territory or one or more specified sectors within a third country, or an international organisation, offers an adequate level of protection within the meaning of Article 45(1), by reason of its domestic law or its international commitments, irrespective of whether the country is bound by a Charter of Fundamental Rights, for the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data, as well as the existence and effective functioning of one or more independent supervisory authorities in that third country..."

Article 50 governs international data transfers. The European Commission can issue "adequacy decisions" — recognising that a third country offers adequate data protection. Adequacy decisions exist for: Andorra, Argentina, Canada (commercial), Faroe Islands, Guernsey, Israel, Isle of Man, Japan, Jersey, New Zealand, Republic of Korea, Switzerland, United Kingdom, Uruguay, and the EU-US Data Privacy Framework.

The Schrems II decision (CJEU, 16 July 2020) invalidated the EU-US Privacy Shield, holding that US surveillance laws did not offer adequate protection. This led to the EU-US Data Privacy Framework (2023), which provides a new mechanism for transatlantic data flows.

📖 Chapter 8 — Remedies, Liability and Penalties (Articles 77-84)

Article 82 — Right to Compensation and Liability

Any person who has suffered material or non-material damage as a result of an infringement of the GDPR has the right to receive compensation.

Article 83 — General Conditions for Imposing Administrative Fines

"5. infringements of the following provisions shall be subject to administrative fines up to 20 000 000 EUR, or in the case of an undertaking, up to 4 % of the total worldwide annual turnover of the preceding financial year, whichever is higher: (a) the principles for processing, including conditions for consent, pursuant to Articles 5, 6, 7 and 9; (b) the data subjects' rights pursuant to Articles 12 to 22; (c) the transfers of personal data to a recipient in a third country or an international organisation pursuant to Articles 44 to 49..."

Maximum fine: €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher.

🌍 The GDPR Effect — Global Privacy Law Convergence

The GDPR has had a global "Brussels Effect" — making it the de facto international standard for data protection. Many countries have adopted GDPR-equivalent laws: UK GDPR (post-Brexit), LGPD (Brazil), PIPL (China), LGPD (Brazil), POPIA (South Africa), APPI (Japan), PIPA (Korea), Privacy Act (Australia), APEC Cross-Border Privacy Rules. The CCPA (California) and CPRA (California) explicitly reference GDPR concepts.

The EU AI Act (in force 1 August 2024) builds on the GDPR framework. It uses GDPR concepts of "high-risk" and "prohibited" AI practices, and Article 50 of the EU AI Act is sovereign watermarking — directly derived from GDPR Article 50's adequacy principle.
