📖 Preamble — The Convention Parliament and the Abdication of James II

The Bill of Rights 1689 was the constitutional settlement that ended the Stuart crisis. It was enacted by the Convention Parliament — the assembly called by William of Orange after his successful invasion of England in November 1688 — and it codified the terms on which William III and Mary II would jointly hold the English throne.

James II of England (James VII of Scotland) had alienated his subjects by a series of unconstitutional acts: the use of the dispensing and suspending powers to exempt Catholics from the Test Acts, the prosecution of the Seven Bishops (June 1688), the expansion of a standing army in peacetime, the issue of a second Declaration of Indulgence (April 1688) read in every church, and the birth of a Catholic heir (James Francis Edward, June 1688) that threatened a permanent Catholic monarchy.

On 5 November 1688, William of Orange landed at Brixham with an invading force of approximately 15,000 men. James's army melted away. On 11 December 1688, James attempted to flee but was intercepted and returned to London. On 23 December 1688, he succeeded in escaping to France, where he was received by Louis XIV. The throne was vacant.

The Convention Parliament met on 22 January 1689. After weeks of debate — in which the Whig case for "abdication" was put by Henry Powle, John Somers, and Sir Robert Howard — the Commons resolved on 28 January 1689 that "King James the Second, having endeavoured to subvert the constitution of the kingdom by breaking the original contract between king and people... has violated the fundamental laws, and withdrawn himself out of the kingdom." The throne was declared vacant. The Crown was offered jointly to William and Mary.

The Declaration of Right — drafted by a committee chaired by Somers, with major contributions from Henry Carey, Viscount Falkland, and Sir George Treby — was presented to William and Mary on 13 February 1689. They accepted, and were crowned on 11 April 1689. The Declaration was enacted as the Bill of Rights on 16 December 1689 by 1 Wm. & Mary sess. 2 c. 2.

📜 The Thirteen Articles of the Bill of Rights (1689)

Reproduced here from the statute roll (with modernised orthography):

Article 1 — The Suspending Power is Illegal

"That the pretended power of suspending of laws, or the execution of laws, by regal authority, without consent of Parliament, is illegal."

The Crown may not unilaterally suspend statutes. This is the death of the suspending power James II had claimed under the Royal Prerogative.

Article 2 — The Dispensing Power is Illegal

"That the pretended power of dispensing with laws, or the execution of laws, by regal authority, as it hath been assumed and exercised of late, is illegal."

The Crown may not unilaterally dispense individuals from the operation of statutes — particularly the Test Acts.

Article 3 — No Standing Army in Peacetime Without Parliament's Consent

"That the commission for erecting the late Court of Commissioners for Ecclesiastical Causes, and all other commissions and courts of like nature, are illegal and pernicious."

The Ecclesiastical Commission that had disciplined the Seven Bishops was abolished.

Article 4 — No Levying Money Without Parliamentary Grant

"That levying money for or to the use of the Crown, by pretence of prerogative, without grant of Parliament, for longer time, or in other manner than the same is or shall be granted, is illegal."

Reaffirms Magna Carta Clause 12 — no taxation without representation.

Article 5 — Subjects Have the Right to Petition

"That it is the right of the subjects to petition the King, and all commitments and prosecutions for such petitioning are illegal."

The freeborn Englishman's right to petition is affirmed.

Article 6 — No Standing Army Without Parliament's Consent

"That the raising or keeping a standing army within the kingdom in time of peace, unless it be with consent of Parliament, is against law."

The Crown may not maintain a peacetime army without Parliamentary approval. The Mutiny Act 1689 regularised the army by annual parliamentary grant.

Article 7 — Protestant Subjects May Bear Arms

"That the subjects which are Protestants may have arms for their defence suitable to their conditions, and as allowed by law."

The right of Protestants to bear arms — later extended to all subjects by the 19th century.

Article 8 — Parliamentary Elections Must Be Free

"That elections of members of Parliament ought to be free."

No royal interference in parliamentary elections.

Article 9 — Parliamentary Freedom of Speech

"That the freedom of speech, and debates or proceedings in Parliament, ought not to be impeached or questioned in any court or place out of Parliament."

The foundation of Article IX of the U.S. Constitution. MPs may speak freely without fear of prosecution.

Article 10 — No Excessive Bail, Fines, or Cruel Punishments

"That excessive bail ought not to be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted."

The Eighth Amendment (U.S.) descends almost verbatim from this article.

Article 11 — Jury Trials and Grants of Fines

"That jurors ought to be duly impanelled and returned, and jurors which pass upon men in trials for high treason ought to be freeholders."

Juries for high treason must consist of freeholders — protecting against packed London juries.

Article 12 — No Forfeitures Without Conviction

"That all grants and promises of fines and forfeitures of particular persons before conviction are illegal and void."

The Crown may not promise the profits of a man's conviction before the trial — preventing prosecutorial corruption.

Article 13 — Frequent Parliaments

"And that for redress of all grievances, and for the amending, strengthening, and preserving of the laws, Parliaments ought to be held frequently."

Parliaments must be held at least triennially — established by the Triennial Act 1694.

In addition, the Act contained the formal abdication of James II, the offer of the throne to William and Mary, and the coronation oath — the King must "govern the people of this kingdom of England, and the dominions thereunto belonging, according to the statutes in Parliament agreed on, and the laws and customs of the same."

📜 The Toleration Act 1689

Passed on the same day as the Bill of Rights, the Toleration Act (1 Wm. & Mary c. 18) granted freedom of worship to Protestant nonconformists (though not to Catholics or Unitarians, who were excluded). The Test Acts and Sacramental Test remained in force for public office. This is the foundation of religious liberty in England — distinct from religious equality.

🇺🇸 American Adoption

The U.S. Constitution's First Amendment (1791) descends from Article 9 (parliamentary freedom of speech); the Second Amendment from Article 7 (right to bear arms); the Fifth Amendment's grand jury clause from Article 11; the Sixth Amendment's jury trial from Article 11; the Eighth Amendment from Article 10 verbatim.

The framers of the Bill of Rights 1791 — drafted by Madison in 1789 — explicitly cited the English Bill of Rights as their model. James Madison's notes show that he had the 1689 text open before him as he drafted each of the first ten amendments.
