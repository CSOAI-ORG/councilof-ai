Preamble — the Putney Debates, October–November 1647

The Levellers were the most radical political movement of the English Civil War. In the Church of St Mary the Virgin, Putney, between 28 October and 11 November 1647, the Council of the Army debated the franchise, religious toleration, and the future of the English state. The debates were transcribed by William Clarke, Secretary to the Council, and constitute the most vivid verbatim record of any pre-modern political assembly. Three movements — the Levellers, the High Tories (Grandees), and the Anabaptists under Coppe — fought for the soul of the New Model Army after the dust settled at Naseby.

Preconditions — the First Civil War and the New Model Army (1642–1646)

Charles I's defeat of Parliamentary forces at Edgehill (23 October 1642) and his failure at Marston Moor (2 July 1644) produced the Self-Denying Ordinance (3 April 1645) and the formation of the New Model Army under Sir Thomas Fairfax, with Oliver Cromwell as Lieutenant-General of Horse. After the surrender of Oxford in June 1646, the Army numbered ~70,000 men, predominantly Puritan Independents who distrusted the Presbyterian majority in Parliament. Pay was months in arrears; the soldiers expected reform.

The Agreement of the People (28 October 1647)

Drafted by John Wildman, with extensive input from John Lilburne, Richard Overton, and William Walwyn, the Agreement of the People was the Levellers' manifesto for a new constitution. It was presented to the Council of the Army on 28 October 1647. The 1647 version is the radical one — later iterations (1649) softened to reflect the regicide and Cromwell's compromises.

Articles 1–3: Sovereignty and Representation

Article I. That the people of England, being, after the dissolution of this Parliament, left without a representative, and that the representatives of the people have declared in their declaration of 17 March 1647, that the people are the original of all just power; and that the people have an unquestionable right to choose their own representatives in Parliament; we do agree and declare that the supreme authority of England and Ireland shall be in this and the successive representatives of the people thereof, in Parliament assembled; and that all courts of justice, all magistrates and officers, all commanders and soldiers, both by sea and land, shall be from time to time constituted, and give an account of their proceedings, to such representatives of the people.
Article II. That the people of England, and of all other nations where the people of England have any jurisdiction or dominion, shall be no longer represented in any way but by their own free and voluntary choice; and that no man shall in any case be obliged to appear before any court or magistrate, or to answer any question, except such as shall be by his representative in Parliament freely chosen and agreed unto.
Article III. That the representative of the people shall consist of four hundred persons, but no more; to be chosen by the people of England and Wales, in proportion to their numbers, after the rate of one for every four thousand inhabitants; that the people of London and Westminster shall have four; that there shall be a representative for Ireland.

Articles 4–5: Elections and Duration

Article IV. That all and every inhabitant of England and Wales, of the age of twenty-one years and upwards (excepting servants, and those who have been servants to the King or any other person, within three years last past, and excepting such as receive alms, and such as are not assessed to the public taxes of the kingdom) shall have a free and equal voice in the election of their representatives.

Article IV is the radical franchise: universal manhood suffrage for householders and taxpayers — a leap from the 5% adult-male franchise of the Long Parliament. The exceptions (servants, ex-servants of the King, alms-takers, untaxed) reflect the household-economic theory of the era.

Article V. That this representative shall continue for two years at the farthest, and shall meet once every year; that the first representative shall have the supreme authority over all the militia of the kingdom, and shall have the sole power of declaring war and peace; and that all laws and statutes shall be made, repealed, or altered by a majority of the representatives.

Articles 6–9: Mandate, Religious Liberty, Accountability

Article VI. That no member of any representative shall be chosen who hath not been a member of some godly congregation, and signed the Solemn League and Covenant; and that the representatives shall not have power to continue themselves longer than two years.
Article VII. That in all matters of controversy and debate, the voice of every free-born Englishman shall be heard; that the people shall have a right to petition the Parliament, and that no man shall be punished for petitioning.
Article VIII. That all men shall have liberty of conscience in matters of religion, and shall not be forced to maintain any minister against their will; that no man shall be molested for differing in opinion from the established religion.
Article IX. That there shall be a council of state, chosen by the Parliament, to manage the affairs of the Commonwealth, to be subordinate to Parliament and accountable to it.

Articles 10–13: Separation of Powers and Security

Article X. That the representative shall not have power to imprison any man, except in cases of treason, felony, or breach of the peace; that all imprisonments shall be by warrant under hand of the Lord General or chief officer of the army.
Article XI. That there shall be no martial law in any part of the kingdom, except in cases of actual war, and then only in the army; and that no soldiers shall be quartered in private houses against the will of the owners.
Article XII. That all laws, statutes, and ordinances shall be published in every county and parish of the kingdom; and that no man shall be punished except by the law of the land.
Article XIII. That no loans or taxes shall be levied upon the people without the consent of their representatives in Parliament; and that the people shall not be compelled to contribute to any loan, benevolence, or other charge, except by their own free consent in Parliament.

Specific Cases — the Levellers' Martyrs

John Lilburne (1614–1657): the Levellers' "free-born John." Born in Sunderland. Flogged through the streets of Westminster in 1638 for importing Puritan tracts. Acquitted of treason in the Star Chamber (1639), which made him a popular hero. Authored "An Agreement of the Free People of England" (1649). Died in exile in Utrecht, aged 42. Note: the modern "Lilburne doctrine" of press freedom derives from his case.

Richard Overton (fl. 1640–1664): poet and pamphleteer. Authored "An Arrow against all Tyrants" (1646), the most articulate defence of natural rights of the period. Imprisoned in the Tower of London for "An Appeal" (1647). Released in October 1647 under the Vote of No Addresses.

Colonel Robert Lilburne (1613–1665): John's brother; commanded the Eastern Association regiment. The only Leveller-leaning officer of regimental rank.

Colonel John Rainborough (c. 1611–1648): the Army's Lieutenant-General of Foot, raised the Leveller cry at Putney on 29 October 1647 — the famous "I am resolved to give my vote that whatsoever Englishman hath a livelihood, shall have a vote". Killed at Doncaster Bridge (29 October 1648) under suspicious circumstances.

Colonel Thomas Rainsborough (1611–1668): brother of John; commanded the New Model's foot regiment at Naseby. After Putney, ordered to disband the Army but refused.

Modern Application (2026)

The Levellers' charter contains the four principles that became the architecture of every modern Western democracy:

  1. Universal suffrage (Art. IV) — extended through Reform Acts 1832, 1867, 1884, 1918, 1928, and the Equal Franchise Act 1928.
  2. Annual parliaments (Art. V) — softened to 5-year max terms in the Septennial Act 1716, then Parliament Acts 1911.
  3. Religious toleration (Art. VIII) — enacted in the Toleration Act 1689; extended through the Catholic Relief Acts 1829 and 1926.
  4. No taxation without representation (Art. XIII) — the founding cry of the American Revolution (1765), embodied in the US Constitution's Article I, §7 (Origination Clause).

Crown Lineage Position

The Levellers are the 3rd pillar of the 1215–2026 lineage, between the Magna Carta (1215, judicial sovereignty) and the Habeas Corpus Act (1679, due process). Their unique contribution is popular sovereignty — the people as the source of authority, not the monarch or parliament. This principle, suppressed at Putney (Nov 1647) and Burford (May 1649), resurfaced in the Glorious Revolution (1688), the American Declaration (1776), the French Revolution (1789), and every modern democratic constitution.
