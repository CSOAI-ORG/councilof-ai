📜 Preamble

AI ethics is the branch of applied ethics that examines the moral implications of artificial intelligence systems. It draws on philosophy, computer science, law, economics, sociology, and the cognitive sciences. The AI ethics charter synthesizes the foundational international frameworks — UNESCO, OECD, Asilomar AI Principles, EU AI Act, UK AI Bill — and binds them into a single sovereign substrate implementation.

The charter was developed in response to the rapid deployment of AI systems throughout the 2020s — the rise of large language models (LLMs), generative AI, autonomous vehicles, AI weapons, AI governance, AI surveillance, and the use of AI in critical infrastructure. The 2024 EU AI Act (in force 1 August 2024) is the world's first horizontal AI regulation. The 2026 UK AI Bill is the British equivalent. The 2023 Bletchley Declaration was the first global AI safety declaration. The 2024 Seoul Summit and 2025 Paris Summit followed. Together, these instruments form the global AI ethics framework.

📖 Section 1 — The Asilomar AI Principles (2017)

The Asilomar AI Principles were drafted at the Asilomar Conference on Beneficial AI (January 2017) by 119 attendees from academia, industry, and civil society. The 23 principles are divided into three categories: Research, Ethics and Values, and Longer-Term Issues.

Research Issues

  1. Research Goal: The goal of AI research should be to create not undirected intelligence, but beneficial intelligence.
  2. Research Funding: Investments in AI should be accompanied by funding for research on ensuring its beneficial use.
  3. Science-Policy Link: There should be constructive and healthy exchange between AI researchers and policy-makers.
  4. Research Culture: A culture of cooperation, trust, and transparency should be fostered among AI researchers and developers.
  5. Avoid Race Avoidance: Teams developing AI systems should actively cooperate to avoid corner-cutting on safety standards.

Ethics and Values

  1. Safety: AI systems should be safe and secure throughout their operational lifetime, verifiably so where applicable and feasible.
  2. Failure Transparency: If an AI system causes harm, it should be possible to ascertain why, both technically and ethically.
  3. Judicial Transparency: Any involvement by an autonomous system in judicial decision-making should provide a satisfactory explanation auditable by a competent human authority.
  4. Responsibility: Designers and builders of advanced AI systems are stakeholders in the moral implications of their use, misuse, and actions, with a responsibility and opportunity to shape those implications.
  5. Value Alignment: Highly autonomous AI systems should be designed so that their goals and behaviors can be assured to align with human values throughout their operation.
  6. Human Values: AI systems should be designed and operated so as to be compatible with ideals of human dignity, rights, freedoms, and cultural diversity.
  7. Personal Privacy: People should have the right to access, manage and control the data they generate, given AI systems' power to analyse and utilise that data.
  8. Library and Information Access: Access to shared data should not be unduly curtailed by the AI systems' reliance on it.
  9. Freedom and Privacy: The application of AI to personal data must not unreasonably curtail people's real or perceived liberty.
  10. Shared Benefit: AI technologies should benefit and empower as many people as possible.
  11. Shared Prosperity: The economic prosperity created by AI should be shared broadly, to benefit all of humanity.
  12. Human Control: Humans should choose how and whether to delegate decisions to AI systems, to accomplish human-chosen objectives.
  13. Non-subversion: The power conferred by control of highly advanced AI systems should respect and improve, rather than subvert, the social and civic processes on which the health of society depends.
  14. AI Arms Race: An arms race in lethal autonomous weapons should be avoided.

Longer-Term Issues

  1. Recursion Caution: The capability for self-improvement or self-replication of AI systems should be subject to safety precautions.
  2. Common Good: AI should be designed to align with human values and contribute to the common good.
  3. Superintelligence: Superintelligence should be developed in service of widely shared ethical ideals, and for the benefit of all humanity rather than one state or organization.

📖 Section 2 — UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021)

The UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence was adopted by the 193 Member States of UNESCO at the 41st session of the General Conference in Paris, 24 November 2021. It is the first global standard on AI ethics.

The 4 Core Values

  1. Respect, protection and promotion of human rights, fundamental freedoms and human dignity — AI systems must not violate human rights.
  2. Environment and ecosystem flourishing — AI must be environmentally responsible.
  3. Ensuring diversity and inclusiveness — AI must be inclusive.
  4. Building peaceful, just and interconnected societies — AI must support peace and justice.

The 10 Principles

  1. Proportionality: The AI method should be proportional to the problem.
  2. Safety and security: AI systems must be safe and secure.
  3. Fairness and non-discrimination: AI must not discriminate.
  4. Sustainability: AI must be sustainable.
  5. Right to privacy and data protection: Privacy and data protection must be preserved.
  6. Human oversight and determination: Humans must retain oversight and final determination.
  7. Transparency and explainability: AI must be transparent and explainable.
  8. Responsibility and accountability: AI developers are responsible and accountable.
  9. Awareness and literacy: Citizens must be AI-literate.
  10. Multi-stakeholder and adaptive governance and collaboration: AI governance must be multi-stakeholder and adaptive.

📖 Section 3 — OECD AI Principles (2019, Updated 2024)

The OECD AI Principles were the first intergovernmental standard on AI, adopted in 2019 by OECD Member States. They were updated in 2024 to address generative AI.

Five Values-Based Principles

  1. Inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being
  2. Human-centred values and fairness
  3. Transparency and explainability
  4. Robustness, security and safety
  5. Accountability

Five Recommendations to Governments

  1. Facilitate public and private AI R&D investment
  2. Foster an inclusive AI-enabling ecosystem (digital infrastructure, data, skills)
  3. Shape an interoperable governance environment
  4. Build human capacity and prepare for labour market transformation
  5. Build international cooperation for trustworthy AI

📖 Section 4 — The Bletchley Declaration (2023) and AI Safety Summits

The Bletchley Declaration was signed on 1 November 2023 at the AI Safety Summit at Bletchley Park by 28 governments including the UK, USA, China, India, Brazil, and the EU. It is the first global AI safety declaration.

Bletchley Declaration — Key Points

  • AI poses catastrophic risks that demand international cooperation.
  • AI should be developed and deployed in a safe, responsible way.
  • Frontier AI systems pose particular risks that need careful management.
  • Transparency and accountability are essential.

Seoul Summit Declaration (May 2024)

The Seoul Summit produced the Seoul Declaration on AI, signed by 16 nations including the UK, USA, France, Germany, Japan, Korea, India, Canada, Italy, Spain, Brazil, Mexico, Chile, Singapore, Indonesia, and Australia. Key commitments:

  • Frontier AI safety commitments
  • AI safety institutes to be established in each major nation
  • Common safety testing frameworks

Paris AI Action Summit (February 2025)

The Paris AI Action Summit focused on AI for good, sustainability, and inclusion. It produced the Paris Statement on Inclusive and Sustainable AI.

📖 Section 5 — The EU AI Act (2024)

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) is the world's first horizontal AI regulation. It entered into force 1 August 2024. It establishes a risk-based approach to AI regulation.

Risk Categories

  1. Unacceptable risk (Article 5) — banned: social scoring, real-time biometric ID (with exceptions), subliminal manipulation, exploiting vulnerabilities.
  2. High risk (Article 6 + Annex III) — regulated: AI in critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services, law enforcement, migration, justice, biometric identification.
  3. Limited risk (Article 50) — transparency obligations: AI-generated content must be watermarked.
  4. Minimal risk — voluntary codes of conduct.

Article 50 — Transparency Obligations (In force 2 August 2026)

"Providers shall ensure that AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons are designed and developed in such a way that the natural persons concerned are informed that they are interacting with an AI system..."

📖 Section 6 — UK AI Bill (2026)

The UK AI Bill is expected in 2026. It will establish a principles-based, sovereign-aligned approach to AI regulation. The five principles:

  1. Safety, security, robustness
  2. Transparency, explainability
  3. Fairness
  4. Accountability, governance
  5. Contestability, redress

The UK AI Safety Institute (AISI) has statutory footing post-Bletchley. The UK ICO AI Audit Framework (2025 update) provides audit structure.
