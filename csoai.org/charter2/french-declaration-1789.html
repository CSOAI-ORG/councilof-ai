Preamble — the Tennis Court Oath and the August Days

The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (Déclaration des droits de l'homme et du citoyen) was adopted on 26 August 1789 by the National Constituent Assembly, meeting in Versailles. Drafted by the Marquis de Lafayette (with assistance from Thomas Jefferson, then US Minister to France), the Abbé Sieyès, Charles de Talleyrand, and the jurist Nicolas de Condorcet, the document is the French counterpart of the US Declaration (1776) and the English Bill of Rights (1689).

Articles 1–4: Sovereignty and Equality

  1. Article I: Men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Social distinctions may be based only on common utility.
  2. Article II: The purpose of all political association is the preservation of the natural and imprescriptible rights of man. These rights are liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression.
  3. Article III: The principle of any sovereignty resides essentially in the Nation. No body, no individual can exert authority which does not emanate expressly from it.
  4. Article IV: Liberty consists of doing anything which does not harm others: thus, the exercise of the natural rights of every man has no other limits than those which are necessary to secure to every other man the free exercise of the same rights. These limits can only be determined by law.

Articles 5–10: Law and Liberty

  1. Article V: The law has the right to forbid only actions hurtful to society. What is not forbidden by law may not be prevented, and no one may be forced to do what the law does not ordain.
  2. Article VI: Law is the expression of the general will. All citizens have the right to concur personally, or through their representatives, in its formation. It must be the same for all, whether it protects or punishes.
  3. Article VII: No person shall be accused, arrested, or imprisoned except in the cases and according to the forms prescribed by law. Any one soliciting, transmitting, executing, or causing to be executed any arbitrary order shall be punished.
  4. Article VIII: The law shall impose only penalties that are strictly and obviously necessary, and no one shall be punished except by virtue of a law established and promulgated prior to the offence and legally applied.
  5. Article IX: Every man being presumed innocent until he has been declared guilty, if it is deemed indispensable to arrest him, any rigour which is not necessary to secure his person shall be severely reprimanded by the law.
  6. Article X: No one may be disturbed on account of his opinions, even religious, provided their manifestation does not trouble the public order established by law.

Articles 11–17: Free Speech, Force, and Taxation

  1. Article XI: The free communication of ideas and opinions is one of the most precious of the rights of man. Every citizen may therefore speak, write, and print freely, subject to the responsibility for the abuse of this liberty, in cases determined by law.
  2. Article XII: The guarantee of the rights of man and of the citizen requires a public force: this force is therefore instituted for the benefit of all, and not for the private use of those to whom it is entrusted.
  3. Article XIII: A common contribution is indispensable for the maintenance of the public force and for the cost of administration. This should be equitably distributed among all citizens in proportion to their means.
  4. Article XIV: All citizens have the right to ascertain, by themselves or through their representatives, the necessity of the public contribution, to consent to it freely, to watch over its use, and to determine its amount, basis, collection, and duration.
  5. Article XV: Society has the right to demand of every public agent an account of his administration.
  6. Article XVI: Any society in which the guarantee of rights is not assured, nor the separation of powers determined, has no constitution.
  7. Article XVII: Property being an inviolable and sacred right, no one may be deprived of it, except when public necessity, legally ascertained, obviously requires it, and on condition of a just prior indemnity.

Modern Application (2026)

The Declaration has shaped:

  • The French Constitution of 1791, the Third Republic (1870), the Fourth Republic (1946), the Fifth Republic (1958) — all preambular citations.
  • The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) — Articles 1–3 descend directly.
  • The European Convention on Human Rights (1950) — Articles 5, 6, 9, 10, 11, 14 descend directly.
  • The EU Charter of Fundamental Rights (2000).
