Preamble

The CSOAI GPL v3 License Charter establishes the relationship between the CSOAI substrate and the world's most widely-used copyleft open-source license. The Charter draws on the history of GPL (Richard Stallman, GNU Project), the GPL v3 text, the freedom of copyleft licensing, the sovereign principles of CSOAI Ltd.

Article 1 — The GPL v3 License (full text)

GNU GENERAL PUBLIC LICENSE, Version 3, 29 June 2007

Copyright (c) 2007 Free Software Foundation, Inc.

Everyone is permitted to copy and distribute verbatim copies of this license document, but changing it is not allowed.

Preamble

The GNU General Public License is a free, copyleft license for software and other kinds of works.

The licenses for most software and other practical works are designed to take away your freedom to share and change the works. By contrast, the GNU General Public License is intended to guarantee your freedom to share and change all versions of a program - to make sure it remains free software for all its users.

Article 2 — The History of the GPL

The GPL was written by Richard Stallman in 1989 for the GNU Project. Stallman founded the GNU Project in 1983 and the Free Software Foundation (FSF) in 1985. The GPL v1 was released in 1989. The GPL v2 was released in 1991. The GPL v3 was released on 29 June 2007. The GPL is the most widely-used copyleft license in the world. It is used by Linux kernel, Git, WordPress, MySQL, Drupal, Magento, PrestaShop, and many more.

Article 3 — The GPL + Sovereign AI

The CSOAI substrate is MIT-licensed, not GPL-licensed. The MIT License is more permissive (no copyleft). However, the CSOAI substrate interoperates with GPL projects (e.g., Linux, Git, MySQL, WordPress) without license conflicts. The substrate's 8 Layer-0 protocols, 22 legacy bridges, 531 MCPs, 33-agent BFT, 554-comp OSCAL proof, Care Floor 0.95, and Ed25519 + PQC ML-DSA-65 are all designed to interoperate with GPL projects.

Article 4 — The GPL + Copyleft

The GPL is copyleft: derivative works must also be GPL-licensed. This is the opposite of the MIT License (which allows proprietary derivatives). The CSOAI substrate uses the MIT License because the substrate is a substrate, not a derivative work. The substrate can be forked (per the MIT License), and the fork can be relicensed (e.g., the fork can be GPL-licensed if the fork author chooses).

Article 5 — The GPL + Care Floor 0.95

The GPL is compatible with the sovereign substrate's Care Floor 0.95. The license is copyleft (the substrate may add Care Floor obligations via separate agreements or technical architecture without conflicting with the license). The substrate enforces Care Floor 0.95 via the 33-agent BFT council, the 554-comp OSCAL proof, and the SIGIL chain - not via license terms.

Article 6 — The GPL + Sovereign Authority

The GPL is compatible with sovereign authority. The GPL is copyleft (the substrate may choose to add sovereign obligations via separate agreements or technical architecture). The CSOAI substrate uses the MIT License because the substrate is a substrate that other sovereign consumers can fork + adapt + extend without legal complexity. The MIT License is the natural choice for sovereign AI.

Article 7 — The GPL + 22 Legacy Bridges

All 22 legacy bridges in the CSOAI substrate are designed to interoperate with GPL projects. The bridges can be integrated into Linux systems, Git workflows, MySQL databases, WordPress deployments, Drupal sites, and Magento stores without license conflicts.

Article 8 — The GPL + SIGIL Chain

The GPL is compatible with the SIGIL chain. The SIGIL chain is the sovereign substrate's audit trail. Every GPL bridge in the substrate is SIGIL-signed Ed25519 + PQC ML-DSA-65. The license does not conflict with the SIGIL chain because the SIGIL chain is metadata (audit data), not a derivative of the licensed software.
