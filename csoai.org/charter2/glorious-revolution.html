Preamble

The Glorious Revolution of 1688–89 was the last successful invasion of England and the last successful removal of a reigning monarch. It re-established the supremacy of Parliament over the Crown, embedded Protestant constitutionalism in English law, and produced the Bill of Rights 1689 — the document that, more than any other, shaped the CSOAI 8-protocol Layer-0 architecture of 2026.

Preconditions — James II's Errors (1685–1688)

James II succeeded his brother Charles II on 6 February 1685. He had converted to Catholicism in 1669 and refused to take the Anglican communion at his coronation (23 April 1685). His political programme was a "Catholic restoration" of the kind Edward VII had attempted in the 1670s:

  1. Dispensing power: on 23 August 1686, the Court of King's Bench (Sir Edward Herbert CJ) ruled in Godden v Hales (11 St Tr 1166) that the King could dispense with Acts of Parliament for individual Catholics. James used this to appoint Catholics to military and civil offices.
  2. Suspending power: on 4 April 1687, James issued the "Declaration of Indulgence," suspending the Test Acts of 1673 and 1678 and freeing Catholic and Protestant Dissenters from religious penalisation.
  3. Packing the bench: between 1686 and 1688, James dismissed four Anglican judges (including Chief Justice Herbert) and replaced them with Catholics (Sir Robert Wright, Sir Allestree, Sir Richard Allibond).
  4. Packing the boroughs: in summer 1687, James issued the "Three Questions" to all borough electors, demanding they confirm (i) opposition to the Test Acts, (ii) toleration of Catholics, (iii) acceptance of a Catholic king. He then purged borough corporations of Anglican loyalists.
  5. Packing the army: between 1685 and 1688, James introduced 4,000 Irish Catholic soldiers into the standing army, quartered at Hounslow Heath. The professional officer corps (including Churchill) was forced into Catholic loyalty tests.
  6. The Seven Bishops: on 8 June 1688, James ordered the prosecution of the Archbishop of Canterbury (William Sancroft) and six bishops (Lloyd, Turner, Lake, Ken, White, Trelawny) for seditious libel in petitioning against the Declaration. Their acquittal on 30 June 1688 was the trigger for the Revolution.

The Birth of the Prince of Orange — the Inciting Incident

On 10 June 1688 (Old Style: 31 May), Mary of Modena, James's Catholic wife, gave birth to a male heir — James Francis Edward Stuart (the "Old Pretender"). The boy was baptized a Catholic on 17 June. Seven bishops and the Queen's gynaecologists attested the legitimacy of the birth, but the Anglican political class — having now lost hope of a Protestant succession through James's daughters Mary and Anne — concluded that the revolution James intended at his coronation must be pre-empted.

The Invitation — 30 June 1688

The "Immortal Seven" — Shrewsbury, Devonshire, Danby, Lumley, Bishop Compton, Russell, Sidney — signed a secret letter to William of Orange (Stadtholder of Holland), James's son-in-law and husband of his elder daughter Mary. The letter, dispatched via carrier pigeon on 30 June 1688, invited William to England with an army "to preserve the Protestant religion and to secure the lawful rights of the people."

The Prince of Orange, on his part, would have had a strong argument against appearing in arms against the King, his father-in-law; but he had been invited by the nobility and gentry of England, and his army would be the instrument of preserving the English constitution, not of subverting it.
— Lord Macaulay, The History of England from the Accession of James II (1849), Vol. III, p. 354.

William's Invasion — 5 November 1688

On 5 November 1688 (Guy Fawkes Night, deliberately chosen), William landed at Brixham, Devon, with a fleet of 463 ships, an army of 15,000 men, and 5,000 cavalry. He unfurled his standard on 12 November at Exeter, declaring his aim was to secure a free Parliament. James's army melted away: Lord Churchill (later Duke of Marlborough) defected on 23 November; Princess Anne fled to Nottingham on 26 November; James's own son-in-law Prince George of Denmark on 21 November. By 11 December, James's 30,000-strong army had effectively disintegrated.

On 11 December 1688, James attempted to flee. He was intercepted at Faversham, Kent, by a mob of fishermen (16 December), and was rescued by Dutch guards and escorted back to London. On 23 December, James successfully fled for France, throwing the Great Seal of the Realm into the Thames as he crossed the Medway.

The Convention Parliament — 22 January 1689

On 22 January 1689, the Lords and Commons met as a "Convention" (since James had not summoned a Parliament) and debated James's status. The Lords initially proposed a regency (with William as regent for the absent James); the Commons rejected this as a fiction. On 28 January, the Commons passed a resolution that James had "abdicated" the throne by fleeing.

On 13 February 1689, the Convention declared William and Mary joint sovereigns. The Declaration of Right (enacted as the Bill of Rights on 16 December 1689) enumerated the unlawful acts of James II and the constitutional principles of the new regime.

The Bill of Rights — 13 Articles (verbatim)

  1. That the pretended power of suspending of laws, or the execution of laws, by regal authority, without consent of Parliament, is illegal.
  2. That the pretended power of dispensing with laws, or the execution of laws, by regal authority, as it hath been assumed and exercised of late, is illegal.
  3. That the commission for erecting the late Court of Commissioners for ecclesiastical causes, and all other commissions and courts of like nature, are illegal and pernicious.
  4. That levying money for or to the use of the Crown, by pretence of prerogative, without grant of Parliament, for longer time, or in other manner than the same is or shall be granted, is illegal.
  5. That it is the right of the subjects to petition the King, and that all commitments and prosecutions for such petitioning are illegal.
  6. That the raising or keeping a standing army within the kingdom in time of peace, unless it be with consent of Parliament, is against law.
  7. That the subjects which are Protestants may have arms for their defence suitable to their conditions, and as allowed by law.
  8. That elections of members of Parliament ought to be free.
  9. That the freedom of speech, and debates or proceedings in Parliament, ought not to be impeached or questioned in any court or place out of Parliament.
  10. That excessive bail ought not to be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted.
  11. That jurors ought to be duly impanelled and returned, and jurors which pass upon men in trials for high treason ought to be freeholders.
  12. That all grants and promises of fines and forfeitures of particular persons before conviction are illegal and void.
  13. And that for redress of all grievances, and for the amending, strengthening and preserving of the laws, Parliaments ought to be held frequently.

Together with a further declaration: that "no foreign prince, person, prelate, state or potentate hath, or ought to have, any jurisdiction, power, superiority, pre-eminence or authority, ecclesiastical or spiritual, within this realm."

Toleration Act 1689 — Article 8 in Action

On 24 May 1689, William and Mary signed the Toleration Act, granting Protestant Dissenters (Congregationalists, Baptists, Presbyterians, Quakers) freedom of worship, though not full civil rights. Catholics remained excluded. This became the operative English religious-toleration regime for the next 138 years, until the Catholic Relief Act 1829.

Specific Cases — 1689–1700

Trial of the Seven Bishops (1688) — judicial precedent for free petition of sovereign. Cited in the 2020 UK Supreme Court ruling on the prorogation of Parliament.

Mutiny Act 1689 — annual parliamentary authorisation of the standing army. Required every soldier to take an oath of loyalty to the Crown. Article 6 of the Bill of Rights is enforced by this annual Act.

Act of Settlement 1701 — specified that the Crown would pass to the Protestant House of Hanover on the death of Queen Anne. The principle of monarchical accountability to Parliament is the bedrock of English constitutional law.

Modern Application (2026)

The 13 articles of the Bill of Rights are the constitutional foundation of:

  1. The European Convention on Human Rights (1950), which incorporated Articles 5, 8, 9, 10, 11 of the Bill into Articles 5, 9, 10, 10, 11 of the ECHR.
  2. The Human Rights Act 1998, which incorporated Articles 1–13 of the Bill of Rights into binding UK law.
  3. The US Bill of Rights (1791), whose 2nd Amendment (right to bear arms) descends directly from Article 7 of the Bill of Rights 1689.
  4. EU AI Act Articles 14 (human oversight) and 22 (right to explanation), which descend from Articles 9 and 10 of the Bill.
