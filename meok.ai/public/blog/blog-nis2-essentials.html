1. NIS2 Overview: Who's In Scope, What Changed, and the 2026 Enforcement Reality

The NIS2 Directive (Directive EU 2022/2555) represents a fundamental expansion of the EU's cybersecurity regulatory framework. Replacing the original NIS Directive (2016/1148), NIS2 came into force on 16 January 2023, with member states required to transpose it into national law by 17 October 2024. By mid-2026, virtually all member states have completed transposition, and national competent authorities are actively enforcing compliance across the expanded scope.

The most significant change from NIS1 is the expansion of sector coverage. NIS2 applies to 18 sectors divided into two categories:

Essential Entities (most stringent obligations): Energy, Transport, Banking, Financial Market Infrastructures, Health, Drinking Water, Waste Water, Digital Infrastructure, ICT Service Management, Public Administration, and Space. Essential entities are typically large enterprises (250+ employees or €50m+ turnover) in these sectors.

Important Entities (less stringent but still binding): Postal and Courier Services, Waste Management, Manufacture/Production/Distribution of Chemicals, Food, Manufacturing, Digital Providers, and Research. Important entities are medium-sized (50-249 employees or €10m-€50m turnover).

For AI compliance officers, the critical implication is that any organisation deploying AI systems in these sectors is subject to NIS2 — not just because of the AI deployment itself, but because the ICT infrastructure supporting AI (MCP servers, model registries, inference endpoints, cloud providers) falls under NIS2's definition of network and information systems.

The 2026 enforcement reality is that competent authorities are conducting compliance audits, with a particular focus on incident reporting capability, supply chain security, and governance accountability. Organisations that have not yet registered their AI infrastructure with their national NIS2 authority face escalating supervisory measures, including mandatory remediation orders and — in persistent non-compliance cases — fines and personal liability for senior management.

2. The AI Angle: How NIS2 Captures AI Incidents, MCP Infrastructure, and Model Supply Chains

NIS2 does not mention "artificial intelligence" explicitly, but its broad definitions capture AI systems comprehensively. The directive defines "network and information system" as including any electronic communications network, digital device, or interconnected system — which encompasses every layer of the AI technology stack.

AI Incidents Under NIS2

An ICT security incident under NIS2 includes any event that compromises the availability, authenticity, integrity, or confidentiality of stored, transmitted, or processed data. This clearly covers:

  • Model poisoning or backdoor attacks — unauthorised modification of AI model weights or behaviour
  • Data leakage through MCP invocations — customer data exposed via inference output or MCP server logs
  • Adversarial evasion attacks — inputs designed to bypass AI system protections (e.g., prompt injection in a customer-facing chatbot)
  • Training data compromise — data poisoning or corruption affecting model outputs
  • AI service disruption — denial-of-service attacks against inference endpoints or MCP meshes that render AI services unavailable

MCP Infrastructure as a NIS2 System

Each MCP server in your AI estate is a network and information system under NIS2. This means:

  • Every MCP server must be registered with your national NIS2 authority as part of your essential or important entity designation
  • MCP server compromises are reportable incidents within the 24h/72h/1-month timeline
  • MCP server updates and configuration changes are subject to security assessment obligations under Article 21

Model Supply Chain Under NIS2

Article 21(2)(d) explicitly requires entities to assess the cybersecurity of their ICT supply chain, including the security-related aspects of the relationship with each supplier. For AI deployments, this means:

  • Foundation model providers are ICT suppliers under NIS2
  • Cloud inference platforms are ICT suppliers
  • Open-source model repositories and their distribution mechanisms are part of your supply chain
  • MCP server vendors and integration partners are ICT suppliers

Each supplier relationship requires documented security assessment, contractual security obligations, and ongoing monitoring. This is a substantial operational burden for organisations that use multiple AI models from multiple providers — which is the vast majority of enterprise AI deployments in 2026.

3. Incident Reporting in the AI Era: 24h/72h/1-Month Timelines

NIS2 introduces significantly tighter incident reporting timelines than NIS1. For essential entities, the process is:

  • Early warning (24 hours) — An initial notification within 24 hours of becoming aware of a significant incident, including preliminary assessment of severity and impact
  • Notification (72 hours) — A full notification with updated information on the incident's nature, severity, and evolving impact
  • Final report (1 month) — A comprehensive final report including root cause analysis, impact assessment, and remediation actions taken

For important entities, the timeline is slightly longer but still demanding. For AI-specific incidents, these timelines present unique challenges because AI incidents can be difficult to detect in real time. A model poisoning attack may not manifest immediately — the poisoned model may pass initial testing and only produce compromised outputs weeks later.

4. The 2026 Compliance Timeline: Member State Implementation Status

By mid-2026, the NIS2 transposition landscape is largely complete, but material divergences remain between member states. For cross-border AI operators, these differences create a complex compliance map:

  • Most member states have transposed NIS2 on schedule, establishing competent authorities, incident reporting portals, and registration mechanisms for essential and important entities
  • Penalty regimes vary — maximum fines range from €10 million or 2% of global turnover (standard NIS2 ceiling) up to higher national thresholds in some jurisdictions
  • Registration requirements differ — some member states require all essential entities to register their ICT systems (including AI infrastructure) explicitly, while others use a self-declaration model
  • Cross-border incident reporting — entities operating in multiple member states must report to each national competent authority where they have a material presence, creating potential for conflicting requirements

For AI compliance officers, the practical implication is that NIS2 compliance cannot be treated as a single, one-size-fits-all exercise. Your AI attestation and incident reporting infrastructure must be flexible enough to satisfy multiple national frameworks from a single evidence base — which is precisely the design principle behind MEOK's attestation architecture.
