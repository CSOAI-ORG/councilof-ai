1. DORA in a Nutshell: Scope, Timeline, and What's Changed for 2026

The Digital Operational Resilience Act (Regulation EU 2022/2554) entered into full application in January 2025, but 2026 marks the first year that supervisory authorities are conducting systematic DORA compliance assessments across the financial sector. For AI-powered financial services firms, this is the year the regulation bites.

DORA establishes a comprehensive framework for ICT risk management across the EU financial sector, covering credit institutions, investment firms, payment institutions, insurance undertakings, and — critically — ICT third-party service providers. The regulation's five pillars are:

  • ICT Risk Management (Articles 5-16) — Establish, implement, and maintain a sound ICT risk management framework
  • ICT Incident Reporting (Articles 17-23) — Classify, report, and document ICT-related incidents with mandatory notification timelines
  • Digital Operational Resilience Testing (Articles 24-27) — Conduct regular testing including threat-led penetration testing (TLPT) for critical entities
  • ICT Third-Party Risk Management (Articles 28-44) — Manage the risk posed by ICT third-party providers, including cloud services and AI model vendors
  • Information Sharing (Articles 45-47) — Participate in threat intelligence sharing arrangements

For 2026, the key change is enforcement intensity. National competent authorities (ECB for significant institutions, national supervisors for others) are now conducting targeted DORA inspections, with AI systems explicitly in scope. The European Supervisory Authorities (ESAs) have published updated guidelines confirming that AI model serving infrastructure — including MCP meshes, inference pipelines, and model registries — constitutes ICT assets under DORA.

2. Where DORA Meets AI: MCP Servers as ICT Assets

Many financial services firms initially assumed DORA applied only to traditional ICT systems — core banking platforms, payment rails, trading infrastructure. But the regulation's definition of ICT assets is deliberately broad, encompassing "any digital or analogue process, system, service, or infrastructure used by a financial entity to conduct its business."

Your MCP servers are ICT assets under DORA. Specifically:

  • Inference endpoints that serve AI models for credit scoring, fraud detection, and customer personalisation are ICT assets subject to resilience testing under Article 24-27
  • MCP meshes that route AI requests across multiple models are ICT infrastructure requiring business continuity planning under Article 11-12
  • Model registries and deployment pipelines are ICT assets under the change management requirements of Article 10
  • Foundation model API providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, open-source inference providers) are ICT third-party service providers under Title V, subject to contractual oversight and exit planning

The implication is significant. If your banking chatbot invokes an MCP server that calls a large language model hosted by a third-party provider, you have a DORA compliance obligation at every layer: the MCP server itself, the model endpoint, and the third-party provider contract. Each layer must be resilience-tested, incident-reportable, and auditable.

3. ICT Risk Management for AI Systems: Mapping DORA Articles 5-16

The ICT risk management framework under DORA Articles 5-16 requires financial entities to identify, classify, and manage risks across their entire ICT estate. For AI systems, this maps to four specific obligations:

Article 5: ICT Risk Management Framework

Your framework must explicitly cover AI system risks: model drift, adversarial attacks, data poisoning, prompt injection, and inference integrity. The risk taxonomy should classify each MCP invocation by criticality — a fraud-detection model scoring a €1 million wire transfer is a different risk category than a chatbot answering FAQ queries.

Article 9: Protection and Prevention

AI-specific controls include input sanitisation, rate limiting on inference endpoints, model access controls, and cryptographic verification of model outputs. This is where MEOK keystone attestation provides a direct compliance control — each AI interaction is cryptographically signed, creating an irrefutable record of what was served, to whom, and under what security posture.

Article 11: Business Continuity

AI service continuity planning must account for model provider outages, inference capacity constraints, and MCP mesh failures. The 2026 supervisory expectation is that financial entities maintain documented business continuity plans for their AI estate, with alternative model providers or fallback rules identified for each critical AI service.

Article 13: Testing of ICT Systems

Regular testing of AI systems includes: model robustness testing, adversarial resilience assessment, MCP invocation stress testing, and — for critical entities — threat-led penetration testing of AI infrastructure under Article 26-27.

4. Incident Reporting and the Attestation Advantage

DORA incident reporting (Article 17-23) requires financial entities to classify ICT-related incidents within predefined categories (major, significant, or operational) and report them to competent authorities within strict timelines. For AI incidents, this presents a definition challenge: what constitutes an AI-related ICT incident?

Under the ESAs' 2026 guidance, AI-specific reportable incidents include:

  • Model poisoning — unauthorised modification of model weights or behaviour affecting financial decisions
  • Data leakage via MCP — unintended exposure of customer data through MCP invocation logs or inference outputs
  • Adversarial attacks — prompt injection, evasion attacks, or model inversion that compromises AI system integrity
  • Service degradation — AI inference latency or accuracy degradation that impacts financial service availability
  • Third-party AI provider failures — outages or security incidents at foundation model providers that cascade into your services

5. Third-Party ICT Risk: Managing AI Model Providers Under DORA Title V

Title V of DORA (Articles 28-44) introduces stringent requirements for managing ICT third-party risk. For AI-powered financial services, this is arguably the most operationally demanding section of the regulation, because the AI supply chain is complex and often opaque.

Key obligations under Title V that directly affect AI operations:

  • Article 28: Contractual Arrangements — Contracts with AI model providers must include service level commitments, incident notification procedures, audit rights, and exit strategies. Standard API terms of service do not satisfy DORA requirements.
  • Article 29: Register of Information — Financial entities must maintain a register of all ICT third-party arrangements, including AI model providers, inference API vendors, and MCP infrastructure providers. This register must be updated continuously and available to supervisors on request.
  • Article 31: Sub-contracting — If your AI provider uses sub-processors (e.g., a foundation model API that runs on a cloud provider), DORA requires full transparency and contractual oversight of the sub-contracting chain.

The practical implication is that every AI model you use — whether from a major foundation model provider, an open-source inference platform, or a specialist fintech AI vendor — requires a DORA-compliant contractual framework. The MEOK attestation ledger supports this by providing independent verification of your AI providers' service delivery, creating an evidence trail that satisfies both your ICT risk management obligations and your contractual oversight responsibilities.
